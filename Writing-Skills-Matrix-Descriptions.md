# Writing Skills Matrix Descriptions

**A guide for writing the Knowledge and Application descriptions that appear on learner portfolios.**

---

## Why Descriptions Matter

Every description you write appears in three places:

1. **The learner's Skills Portfolio** — the interactive page at `/dashboard/skills`
2. **The printed PDF** — the document a learner downloads or prints
3. **The QR verification page** — the public page anyone can access by scanning the QR code

In all three contexts, the reader is evaluating competence. The reader might be the learner themselves, their employer, a prospective employer, a professional body reviewing CPD evidence, or a colleague deciding whether to involve them in a project.

The descriptions are not internal admin notes. They are public-facing professional statements about what someone knows and what they can do.

---

## The Two Types of Description

### Knowledge Description (Per Course)

- **Answers the question:** "What does this person now understand?"
- **Triggered when:** The learner completes the linked course in LearnWorlds
- **Displayed in:** A purple box under the "Knowledge" heading, beneath the course name
- **Accompanied by:** Course name, completion date, progress score, learning hours

### Application Description (Per Tool)

- **Answers the question:** "What can this person now do?"
- **Triggered when:** The learner completes at least one workflow with the linked tool
- **Displayed in:** A blue box under the "Application" heading, beneath the tool name
- **Accompanied by:** Tool name, project count, last used date

## Core Principles

### 1. Describe the learner, not the content

The description should read as a statement about a person's competence, not as a course syllabus or tool feature list.

| Wrong (describes the content) | Right (describes the learner) |
|---|---|
| "This course covers the fundamentals of international tax treaties and their application." | "Has demonstrated understanding of international tax treaty frameworks, including residence determination, permanent establishment thresholds, and treaty benefit entitlements." |
| "This tool calculates withholding tax rates across jurisdictions." | "Can determine applicable withholding tax rates for cross-border payments, applying treaty relief provisions where available." |

### 2. Be specific about the competence

Vague descriptions add no value. If someone reads "Understands transfer pricing" on a portfolio, they learn nothing they couldn't have guessed from the category name. Specify *what aspect* of the subject the learner has demonstrated.

| Too vague | Specific enough |
|---|---|
| "Understands VAT." | "Has demonstrated understanding of VAT place-of-supply rules for services, including the B2B reverse charge mechanism and the use-and-enjoyment override." |
| "Can use the compliance tracker." | "Can assess multi-jurisdictional filing obligations against regulatory deadlines, identify overdue items, and prioritise remediation based on risk severity." |

### 3. Match the level to what was actually assessed

The description should reflect what the course or tool actually tested, not what you wish it tested. A short introductory course should not produce a description that implies deep expertise.

| Overstated | Appropriately scoped |
|---|---|
| "Is qualified to prepare and file CRS reports for financial institutions." | "Has demonstrated understanding of CRS classification principles and the self-certification requirements for account holders." |
| "Can prepare a complete transfer pricing documentation package for multinational groups." | "Can identify the required components of a local file and master file, and can populate key sections using comparable data." |

### 4. Use professional register

The descriptions appear alongside completion scores, project counts, and QR verification links. They should read like professional competence statements — the kind you might find on a skills matrix from a training provider, a professional body's competence framework, or a CPD record.

**Appropriate register:**
- "Has demonstrated understanding of..."
- "Can identify, assess, and apply..."
- "Is able to perform... and interpret the results in the context of..."

**Inappropriate register:**
- "Great job completing this course!"
- "You now know about..."
- "Well done — you've used this tool X times"

## Sentence Patterns

The following patterns work well for professional competence descriptions. Use them as starting points, not rigid templates.

### Knowledge Description Patterns

**Pattern 1: "Has demonstrated understanding of [specific topic areas]."**

> Has demonstrated understanding of permanent establishment risk factors, including fixed place of business tests, dependent agent rules, and the impact of digital services on PE exposure.

Best for: Courses that teach foundational concepts.

---

**Pattern 2: "Understands [framework/regulation] as it applies to [specific context]."**

> Understands the OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines as they apply to the selection and application of the most appropriate transfer pricing method for intercompany transactions.

Best for: Courses focused on a specific regulatory framework.

---

**Pattern 3: "Can explain [process/mechanism] including [key components]."**

> Can explain the end-to-end FATCA reporting process, including account holder classification, withholding obligations, and the role of Intergovernmental Agreements in modifying direct reporting requirements.

Best for: Courses that cover a multi-step process or workflow.

---

**Pattern 4: "Has completed training on [topic], covering [scope areas]."**

> Has completed training on cross-border VAT compliance, covering registration thresholds, distance selling rules, the One Stop Shop mechanism, and reverse charge obligations for B2B services.

Best for: Broader courses that span several sub-topics within a domain.

---

### Application Description Patterns

**Pattern 1: "Can perform [specific task] including [key steps or judgements]."**

> Can perform arm's length margin analysis, including gross margin, operating margin, cost-plus markup, and Berry ratio calculations, and can interpret results against comparable benchmarks.

Best for: Calculator-type tools with defined computational steps.

---

**Pattern 2: "Can assess [situation] and determine [outcome], applying [relevant rules]."**

> Can assess whether a jurisdiction qualifies for simplified compliance treatment by applying de minimis, effective tax rate, and routine profit tests against transitional thresholds.

Best for: Assessment or qualification tools where the learner makes a determination.

---

**Pattern 3: "Can complete [document/form type] across [sections/components], including [specific elements]."**

> Can complete a standardised information return across all required sections, including group-level disclosures, entity structure definitions, and jurisdiction-level income and tax computations.

Best for: Form-type tools or document generators.

---

**Pattern 4: "Can evaluate [options/candidates] against [criteria] and recommend [outcome]."**

> Can evaluate candidate filing entities against operational readiness criteria — including jurisdictional implementation status, team capability, systems integration, and advisory support — and recommend the most suitable entity for the filing role.

Best for: Assessment or decision-support tools.

---

**Pattern 5: "Can manage [process] by tracking [items] against [standards/deadlines]."**

> Can manage audit file preparation by tracking documentation completeness across all required sections, identifying critical gaps, and prioritising remediation against filing deadlines.

Best for: Tracker and checklist tools.

---

**Pattern 6: "Can identify [applicable rules/rates] for [scenario type] using [method/source]."**

> Can identify applicable withholding tax rates for cross-border dividend, interest, and royalty payments by reference to domestic rates and bilateral treaty provisions.

Best for: Search and reference tools.

## Length Guidelines

| Context | Recommended Length | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Knowledge Description** | 1–2 sentences (20–50 words) | Appears in a compact purple box alongside score and date. Must be scannable. |
| **Application Description** | 1–2 sentences (15–40 words) | Appears in a compact blue box alongside project count. Slightly shorter than Knowledge since it's paired with quantitative evidence. |

Longer descriptions are not truncated — they display in full — but they disrupt the visual balance of the portfolio. If a description needs more than two sentences, consider whether you're describing the content rather than the competence.

## Knowledge vs Application: Avoiding Overlap

A common mistake is writing Knowledge and Application descriptions that say the same thing in different words. They should complement each other, not repeat.

**The distinction:**

| Knowledge (what they understand) | Application (what they can do) |
|---|---|
| Understands the theory, rules, and framework | Can apply the theory in a practical workflow |
| Passive/conceptual: "understands", "can explain", "has demonstrated understanding" | Active/practical: "can perform", "can assess", "can complete", "can determine" |
| Evidenced by course completion and score | Evidenced by project count and tool usage |

**Example pair for the same skill category:**

> **Knowledge:** Has demonstrated understanding of the jurisdictional effective tax rate calculation, including the treatment of GloBE income adjustments and covered tax components under the OECD model rules.
>
> **Application:** Can calculate jurisdictional ETR from financial inputs, apply substance-based exclusions with year-specific transitional rates, and compute resulting top-up tax liability.

The Knowledge description says they understand the concepts. The Application description says they can execute the calculations. Together, they prove both dimensions.

## Descriptions for Multi-Course and Multi-Tool Categories

When a skill category contains several courses or several tools, each description should focus on what *that specific course or tool* contributes — not repeat the category-level message.

### Multiple Courses in One Category

If a category like "International Tax Compliance" has three linked courses:

| Course | Knowledge Description |
|---|---|
| Course A (Fundamentals) | "Has demonstrated understanding of the core principles of international tax compliance, including residence-based taxation, source-based taxation, and the role of bilateral treaties in eliminating double taxation." |
| Course B (Reporting) | "Has demonstrated understanding of cross-border reporting obligations, including CRS, FATCA, and DAC6 frameworks, and can explain when each regime applies." |
| Course C (Risk Management) | "Has demonstrated understanding of compliance risk assessment for multinational structures, including permanent establishment exposure, transfer pricing audit triggers, and voluntary disclosure procedures." |

Each description is distinct and specific to that course's contribution.

### Multiple Tools in One Category

If the same category has two linked tools:

| Tool | Application Description |
|---|---|
| Tool A (Rate Calculator) | "Can determine applicable withholding tax rates for cross-border payments by jurisdiction pair, distinguishing between domestic and treaty rates." |
| Tool B (Reporting Tracker) | "Can manage multi-regime reporting timelines across CRS, FATCA, and DAC6, tracking filing status and identifying overdue obligations." |

Each description focuses on the distinct skill that tool practises.

---

## Descriptions for Shared Tools

A tool may be linked to more than one skill category. When it is, write a different Application Description for each category, emphasising the aspect of the tool relevant to that category's subject.

**Example:** A general compliance tracker linked to both "VAT Compliance" and "CRS Reporting" categories:

| Category | Application Description |
|---|---|
| VAT Compliance | "Can track VAT registration and filing obligations across multiple jurisdictions, monitoring threshold proximity and deadline compliance." |
| CRS Reporting | "Can manage CRS reporting workflows by tracking account classification status, self-certification collection, and XML submission deadlines." |

Same tool, different competence framing.

---

## Final Checklist Before Saving

For each description you've written, confirm:

- [ ] It describes the **learner's competence**, not the course content or tool features
- [ ] It is **specific** — someone reading it knows what the person can actually do or explain
- [ ] It is **appropriately scoped** — it doesn't overstate what was assessed or practised
- [ ] It uses **professional register** — suitable for a CV, CPD record, or employer review
- [ ] It is **1–2 sentences** and fits visually in the portfolio layout
- [ ] It is **distinct** from other descriptions in the same category (no copy-paste)
- [ ] Knowledge descriptions use understanding verbs: "understands", "has demonstrated", "can explain"
- [ ] Application descriptions use doing verbs: "can perform", "can assess", "can complete", "can determine"
